Picture-based learning aid / Mwongozo wa picha

Compare what you see on the farm.

Original illustrative drawings help you describe visible signs before asking an extension officer or veterinarian. These are illustrations, not photographs or AI diagnoses; real symptoms overlap. Compare the pattern, take your own clear photos, and seek confirmation before treatment.

CROPS / MAZAO

Maize leaves with holes

Holes, scraped patches, frass, or caterpillars may suggest chewing pests such as fall armyworm, but other causes are possible.

Check: inside the whorl, both sides of leaves, number of plants affected and whether damage is spreading. Do not spray until the pest is identified and a locally approved product is selected.

CROPS / MAZAO

Yellowing or wilting leaves

Can result from water stress, nutrient imbalance, root damage, pests or disease; leaf colour alone cannot identify the cause.

Check: soil moisture and drainage, affected areas, roots if safe, leaf undersides, recent fertilizer use and weather. Avoid adding more fertilizer or pesticides by guesswork.

POULTRY / KUKU

Chicken breathing or sudden illness signs

Open-mouth breathing, marked weakness, unusual droppings, nervous signs or sudden deaths can have several infectious and non-infectious causes.

Check: how many birds are sick, age, vaccination history, feed/water, droppings and when it began. Isolate sick birds if safe, limit visitors/equipment sharing and contact a livestock officer urgently for sudden deaths.

DAIRY / MAZIWA

Cow udder or milk changes

A hot, swollen, painful udder or clots/abnormal milk may suggest mastitis, but examination and sometimes milk testing are needed.

Check: affected quarter, udder warmth/swelling, milk appearance, cow appetite and temperature. Call a veterinarian promptly. Follow professional milk-withdrawal guidance; never assume milk is safe during treatment.

Do not diagnose by picture alone. Many diseases look alike, and the correct treatment depends on the actual cause. For severe animal illness or sudden deaths, contact a qualified veterinarian. For crop issues, contact a county agricultural extension officer or trusted Kenyan plant-health service.
